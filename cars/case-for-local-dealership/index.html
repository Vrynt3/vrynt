# The Case for the Local Dealership in a Direct-to-Consumer World Buying a car straight from the manufacturer gets sold as the obvious upgrade: cut out the middleman, click a button, pay one price, done. Tesla built a brand on it, and a wave of EV startups has followed. It's a clean pitch. But "cut out the middleman" only sounds good until you understand what the middleman actually does. I've spent years inside the car business, so here's the honest case — for why the franchised, locally owned dealership still serves buyers and their communities better than a direct-to-consumer model, even as the direct model gets all the hype. ## First, the fair part The direct-to-consumer experience gets real things right, and pretending otherwise would be dishonest. Buyers who hate negotiating love a single, fixed, no-haggle price. The online checkout is smooth. The experience is consistent from one buyer to the next, because there's exactly one seller setting the terms. If your only goal is a frictionless transaction, the direct model delivers it. The question is what you're giving up to get it — and most of what you give up doesn't show up until *after* the sale. ## The "middleman" is competition Here's the part the pitch leaves out. Under the franchise system, a single brand is sold by many independently owned dealers who compete with each other. That competition is *your* leverage. You can play one store against another, shop your trade, and negotiate, because no single dealer controls the price. Direct-to-consumer erases that. The manufacturer sets one number nationwide, and your only options are to accept it or walk. There's no second quote to get, because there's no second seller. A fixed price feels fair right up until you realize "fixed" also means "non-negotiable, by design, in the seller's favor." This isn't an accident of the market — it's baked into law. Every state has laws that limit or ban manufacturers from selling vehicles directly to customers, with many requiring that new cars be sold through franchised dealers. Those laws exist precisely to keep an independent, competitive layer between the automaker and you. ## When something goes wrong, who's actually on your side? This is the one nobody thinks about until they need it. With a franchised dealer, the business that sold you the car is *separate* from the company that built it. If you've got a warranty fight, a recall headache, or a vehicle that's a lemon, your dealer is an independent party who can advocate for you with the manufacturer. Two different companies, and one of them works in your town. Under direct-to-consumer, the seller and the manufacturer are the same entity. When you have a dispute, you're negotiating with the only party in the room — and they're on both sides of the table. Service tells the same story. A franchised brand comes with a dense network of local dealers authorized to handle warranty work and recalls. Direct-to-consumer service tends to be far more concentrated, which for plenty of owners means longer drives and longer waits when their car needs attention. A great buying experience doesn't help much if the nearest service center is two hours away. ## A dealership is a neighbor, not a headquarters A local dealership is a small or mid-sized business owned by people who live where you live. The payroll is local. The property and sales taxes stay in the county and fund local services. The Little League banner, the high school booster program, the food-bank drive, the charity golf outing — that sponsorship money comes from businesses rooted in the community, not wired to a corporate headquarters in another state. Direct-to-consumer reroutes all of that to one company's bottom line. The dollars that used to circulate locally — jobs, taxes, sponsorships, the service tech who coaches your kid's team — flow out to a single HQ instead. "Cutting out the middleman" also means cutting out the local employer, the local taxpayer, and the local sponsor. That's not a small thing for the towns these dealerships anchor. ## Answering the "it's just protectionism" charge Critics have a ready response to all of this: that franchise laws are protectionist, designed to shield dealers rather than help consumers. It's worth taking seriously, because there's a grain of truth in it — any established system protects its incumbents, and the legal patchwork can get genuinely absurd. In Texas, for instance, Tesla can operate "galleries" where staff legally can't discuss pricing, offer test drives, or complete a sale, which helps no one. But the deeper point survives the critique. An independent dealer network isn't a tollbooth — it's the thing that creates price competition within a brand, gives you a separate advocate when the manufacturer messes up, and keeps the money and the service local. Strip it away and you don't get a freer market; you get a single company controlling the price, the service, and the dispute process all at once. The intermediary isn't the problem. The intermediary is the competition. ## How far the workaround goes Here's how committed the direct model is to getting around these laws: where states won't grant an exception, Tesla has started selling on Native American tribal land. Because tribal nations are sovereign and their land falls under federal rather than state law, state franchise rules simply don't reach there. Tesla opened its first such store in 2021 in Nambé Pueblo, New Mexico — fittingly, in a repurposed former casino — and has since lined up showrooms on the Mohegan Tribe's land in Connecticut and the Oneida Indian Nation's land right here in upstate New York. To be clear, that's the tribes' sovereign right, and the partnership is entirely theirs to make — no quarrel there. The issue is what it says about the model. More than thirty manufacturers — Toyota, Ford, all of them — operate inside the same state franchise laws. Tesla, denied an exception, instead routes around them by finding the one patch of ground in the state where those laws don't apply. It's the same sovereignty play that put casinos outside state gambling rules. Whatever you want to call it, it isn't simply "we made car buying easier." It's a company going to remarkable lengths to be the one player that doesn't have to follow the rules everyone else does. ## Bottom line Direct-to-consumer trades away three things that quietly matter: the price competition that gives you leverage, an independent advocate for when the car needs warranty work or the deal goes sideways, and a local business that reinvests in the community around it. What you get in return is a slicker checkout screen. For some buyers, that trade is worth it. For most, once you look past the buying experience to everything that happens after — the service, the support, the accountability, the local roots — the dealership down the road is still doing more for you than a button on a manufacturer's website ever will.
