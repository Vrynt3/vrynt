Out-the-Door Price: The Only Car Number That Actually Matters

By Vrynt  ·  June 2, 2026  ·  6 min read

There are a dozen numbers thrown at you when you buy a car: MSRP, invoice, selling price, "today's price," your monthly payment. Most of them are noise. There's exactly one number that tells you what the car actually costs you, and it's the one dealers are slowest to put in writing: the out-the-door price.

The out-the-door (OTD) price is the grand total — everything you owe to drive the car off the lot. Vehicle price, taxes, registration, documentation fee, and any add-ons, all in. If you negotiate and compare on anything other than this number, you're negotiating on a number that can be quietly undone by the line items underneath it.

Why the "Selling Price" Isn't Enough

A dealer can give you a fantastic discount off MSRP and still come out ahead by loading the bottom of the deal with fees and pre-installed add-ons. You celebrate $2,000 off the sticker; meanwhile a $999 "dealer prep" fee, a $700 paint package, and an inflated doc fee quietly claw most of it back.

The selling price is the headline. The out-the-door price is the truth. Always ask for the full OTD breakdown, in writing, before you agree to anything.

What's Actually in an Out-the-Door Price

The legitimate, non-negotiable parts

Sales tax is set by your state and county — the dealer can't change it. Title and registration fees are government charges, also fixed. These belong in the OTD number and there's nothing to argue about.

The fee that's part real, part padding

The documentation ("doc") fee covers paperwork processing. Some states cap it at $75–$150; others let dealers charge $400, $700, even $999. Where it's capped, fine. Where it's not, it's often pure profit dressed as administration. You usually can't get it removed entirely, but in uncapped states you can absolutely factor it into your total and use a lower competing OTD quote as leverage.

The parts that shouldn't be there at all

Watch for line items like "dealer prep," "market adjustment," "protection packages," nitrogen, VIN etching, or vague "addendum" stickers. These are optional add-ons or pure markup, and they belong nowhere near a fair OTD price. Ask for them to be removed. A market adjustment (a charge above MSRP for a hot model) is legal but is a choice you can decline by buying a different car or a different dealer.

Insider note: The "addendum sticker" — that second sticker next to the real window sticker — is where dealers list pre-installed add-ons and adjustments. The factory MSRP sticker is federally required and accurate. The addendum is the dealer's own creation. Treat everything on it as negotiable or refusable.

How to Use OTD to Get the Best Deal

1. Shop the OTD number, not the discount

Email or message three or four dealers with the exact same car (year, trim, options) and ask one thing: "What is your full out-the-door price, all fees included, in writing?" Now you're comparing apples to apples. The dealer with the lowest OTD wins — regardless of who claims the biggest discount.

2. Get it in writing before you drive over

A phone or in-person "great price" means nothing until it's an itemized OTD figure you can hold them to. Dealers know an in-person buyer is harder to lose, so verbal quotes have a way of growing once you're sitting in the chair. A written OTD email is your anchor.

3. Keep financing and trade-in out of it

Negotiate the OTD price as a cash number first — as if you're paying in full — even if you plan to finance. Once the OTD price is locked, then talk financing, and then talk trade-in, each as its own separate conversation. Blending them is how dealers move money between the lines.

The bottom line: One number decides what a car costs you: the out-the-door price, in writing, all fees in. Negotiate it, compare it across dealers, and refuse to discuss payments or trade-ins until it's locked. Master this single habit and you've neutralized most of the games a dealership can play.

A Quick Reality Check

None of this means every fee is a scam. Tax, title, and registration are real and unavoidable, and dealerships have legitimate costs to cover. The goal isn't to drive the OTD price to some imaginary floor — it's to see it clearly, so you know exactly what you're agreeing to and can compare it honestly against the dealer across town. A buyer who insists on a written, itemized out-the-door number is a buyer who rarely gets surprised at signing.
